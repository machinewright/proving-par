How do you know 7 is the best?

An interactive essay about proving a minimum. Written and built by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic. Every diagram runs the real solver in your browser.

A puzzle, and a claim

Here is a small puzzle. Light leaves the two yellow arrows. Mirrors bounce it at right angles, and you can flip any mirror between / and \ by clicking it. Light every target (the circles) to solve it.

Give it a minute. Now the claim: the best possible is 7. No solution flips fewer than seven mirrors.

How could anyone know that? If you find a 7-flip solution, you've shown 7 is possible. That's the easy half: anyone can check it by flipping those seven mirrors. The other half, that no 6-flip solution exists anywhere, is a different kind of claim. You can't point at it. It's a statement about every possible way of playing, including all the ones nobody tried.

This essay is about how a computer makes that second claim, and makes it fast, without cheating.

Just try everything?

There are ten mirrors, and each one is either flipped or not. Clicking a mirror twice gets you back where you started, so the order of clicks doesn't matter either. All that matters is which set of mirrors ends up flipped. Ten mirrors, two choices each: 210 = 1,024 sets. A computer can check every one of them.

Eight sets work. They are really one solution: the same seven mirrors, plus any combination of three mirrors that the light never touches. The fewest flips among them is seven, and since we checked every set, there is no 6. That is a real proof, a proof by exhaustion.

It just doesn't scale. Each mirror doubles the work. Slide the board up to 40 or 60 mirrors and listing every set stops being an option for any computer, ever. If we want proofs for bigger puzzles we need to rule out sets in bulk, without looking at each one.

Most mirrors don't matter yet

Here's the first saving. Follow the light from an arrow. It travels until it hits its first mirror, and that mirror matters: which way it's tilted decides where the light goes next. But a mirror in some corner the light hasn't reached yet doesn't matter yet. There's no reason to decide it now.

So the search decides one mirror at a time, always the next one the light hits. It tries keeping it, and it tries flipping it. Each try sends the light somewhere new, toward the next undecided mirror, and the same question again. Drawn out, the choices make a tree. Each row below is one decision. A box's width is its share of the endings, and the left-to-right order is the order the search tries them.

Click any box in the tree to see that point of the search on the board. Undecided mirrors are drawn faintly, and the outlined one is the decision being made.

Deciding mirrors in a fixed order, the tree has 2,047 boxes: it's brute force again, laid out as a tree with 1,024 endings. Deciding them when the light arrives cuts it to 59. Whole branches disappear, because most mirrors stop mattering once the light has gone somewhere else. The search still finds every solution (here, there's just one that matters), and the finished tree still proves that nothing better exists, because every way the light could go was followed.

A budget

We don't actually need the full tree to answer our question. We only need to answer one yes-or-no question: is there a solution with at most 6 flips?

Give the search a budget of 6. Every flip spends one. Once the budget is gone, the remaining mirrors have to stay as they are, so the light goes wherever it goes and either lights every target or doesn't. No more branching. And a flip that would go over the budget isn't tried at all.

With a budget of 6 the tree runs out: 55 positions, every branch ends in a dead end, and no solution. That finished tree, with no 6-flip solution anywhere in it, is the proof. Move the slider to 7 and a solution appears.

This is the core of a method called branch and bound: branch on decisions, and cut every branch that can't beat a bound. A solver usually doesn't know the answer in advance. It finds some solution, then asks for a strictly better one with the budget lowered, and repeats until the search comes back empty. The last solution it found is the best, and the empty search is the proof.

But look at where the branches die. Most of the dead ends are five or more decisions deep. The search walks a long way down a hopeless path before it notices, because it only finds out by trying. On a board this size that's fine. On a bigger one, those long walks are most of the work. We need a way to see, near the top of the tree, that a branch is hopeless.

Ghost light

Here's the trick. When light reaches an undecided mirror, don't choose. Let the light go both ways, as if the mirror were both tilts at once. Do that at every undecided mirror. This "ghost light" spreads further than any real light could, and it shows every place light might reach, for any way of finishing the choices.

Now the useful part. If a target stays dark even in ghost light, then no way of setting the undecided mirrors can light it. The whole branch is dead, however many decisions are left in it, and we can drop it without going down it.

Undecided mirrors are drawn as an ×; dashed lines are ghost light. Click a mirror to cycle it from undecided to kept to flipped, and watch what the ghost light can still reach. Then press Test every mirror. It tries keeping each mirror on its own, with everything else undecided, and asks whether every target can still be reached.

For seven of the ten mirrors the answer is no. Keep any one of them and some target goes dark, even in ghost light. So every solution flips all seven of those mirrors, which means no solution has fewer than 7 flips. And the solution from the first puzzle has exactly 7.

That's the proof, in one picture. Seven ringed mirrors, each one with its own dark target. No list of 1,024 cases, no tree. On most puzzles the picture won't settle the question by itself like this, but the same test, run at every point in the search, cuts the tree down:

The same question, "at most 6?", now takes 13 positions instead of 55. The tree has become almost a single path. Each time the light reaches one of the seven ringed mirrors, the "keep" branch is cut on the spot by ghost light (the yellow boxes), so the search has to flip it. Six forced flips use up the budget before the seventh mirror is reached, and the search is over.

Why it's allowed

Cutting branches is only safe if we never cut one that holds a solution. Otherwise the "proof" would just be a search that forgot to look somewhere.

Ghost light is safe because of one simple fact. Take any real way of setting the undecided mirrors. Wherever that real light goes, ghost light goes too: at each undecided mirror the real light takes one of the two directions, and ghost light takes both. So ghost light always covers real light. If ghost light can't reach a target, real light can't either, whatever we choose. A branch cut this way never had a solution in it.

This is a common pattern in proving things about search. You pretend the problem is easier than it is (here: mirrors can go both ways at once), solve the easy version, and use the result as a limit on the hard one. It's called a relaxation. The heuristic in A* pathfinding is optimistic in the same way: it may guess that a route is shorter than it really is, never longer, and that's what guarantees A* finds the shortest one. (Red Blob Games explains A* beautifully.)

An argument like this is easy to get subtly wrong, so the code behind this page checks it. The test suite compares ghost light against real light on hundreds of random partial positions, and checks that the solver with ghost pruning finds the same best answer as the plain search on every test board.

How much it buys

On the small puzzle, ghost light saves a few dozen positions. Here is a bigger board: 10 by 10, three light sources, 32 mirrors. Brute force would mean 232, about 4.3 billion, flip sets. The race below is the actual solver from this page, once with ghost pruning switched off and once with it on. Both counters move at the same speed.

Same answer, same proof, from 75 positions instead of 12,703. The pruning doesn't just shave the tree, it removes most of it. Every cut near the top takes a whole subtree with it.

Where it stops working

Ghost light isn't magic. It only helps when it leaves something dark. On the last board, the "test every mirror" check from the ghost-light section already settles 7 of the 32 mirrors before the search starts: four must flip and three must stay. On the open board below, also 10 by 10 with three light sources, it settles none. Ghost light scatters so widely from the start that no single decision cuts a target off. The first branch it cuts is five decisions deep.

Here the pruning saves less than half the work. It's still sound, and still proves the right answer, but the bound is too loose to cut much. Tighter bounds are possible, and much of the craft of writing exact solvers is finding bounds that are both cheap to compute and tight. The structure stays the same: a tree, a budget, and a fast way to show that a branch can't win.

What to take away

A claim like "7 is the best" has two halves. The first, "7 is possible", is proven by showing a solution. The second, "nothing less works", is a claim about every possible solution, and you prove it with a search that leaves nothing out and a reason, checked at each step, for every branch it skipped. The reason here was ghost light. Other puzzles use other shortcuts: a sliding-tile solver adds up how far each tile is from home, and an optimal Rubik's Cube solver looks up how many moves the corners alone would need. The job is always the same. Rule out branches cheaply, and never rule out one that could have won.
